Scientists discovered an ancient machine that could calculate astronomical phenomena.
No electricity. No wires. Just bronze gears.
This is the Antikythera Mechanism — the world's first computer.
In 1901, Greek sponge divers, caught in a storm, dived near the island of Antikythera.
Forty-five metres down, they found a Roman shipwreck.
Among the treasure, they pulled up a lump of corroded bronze.
It sat in a museum in Athens — until someone spotted a gear wheel inside the crust.
A gear. In a two-thousand-year-old object.
Then, in the nineteen-fifties, a scientist named Derek de Solla Price took a closer look.
He said it was a machine — a calculator made of gears.
The truth only came out in two thousand and five.
Scientists brought a giant X-ray scanner into the museum — eight tonnes of it.
For the first time, they could see inside.
More than thirty bronze gears, locked together like a clock.
Turn the hand crank, and pointers moved across the dials.
It tracked the Sun and the Moon through the zodiac.
It predicted eclipses — a cycle of two hundred and twenty-three months.
It even had a dial for the Olympic Games.
All of it, inside a box the size of a shoebox.
Three thousand tiny Greek letters were carved into the bronze — like an instruction manual.
Nothing this advanced would be built again for over a thousand years.
Who built it? We still don't know.
Two thousand years later, this machine still has secrets.
