How were they built... and why?
This statue weighs eighty tonnes. It moved six kilometres — no wheels, no animals, no cranes.
This is Easter Island — the most remote inhabited island on Earth.
The moai — nearly nine hundred giants, carved from 1250 to 1500.
They are not just heads. Under the soil are full bodies, with arms and hands.
Almost all of them were carved in one quarry — Rano Raraku.
Four hundred are still there, frozen mid-work, half-finished in the rock.
The largest never left — twenty-one metres, two hundred and seventy tonnes.
The finished ones had to reach the coast — some six kilometres away.
And nobody ever saw how they did it.
The islanders always said the statues walked — moved by a sacred power.
Scientists said sledges — but the biggest would have needed fifteen hundred people.
Then, eighteen people with three ropes walked a four-tonne replica one hundred metres in forty minutes.
The secret was the shape — they lean forward, with wide bases built to rock.
Critics call it a stunt — and nobody has ever walked an eighty-tonne statue.
Today only fifty moai stand again — every one put back up by modern people.
The islanders always said they walked. Maybe they were right.
